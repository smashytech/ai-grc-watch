The week in brief: Agents get ID checks

China ties AI agents to identity codes as the EU AI Act reaches Northern Ireland and US politics shifts

It was a quiet week for binding AI rules. 1 · 🇨🇳 China · 6 Nov 2026

China requires identity codes and update reviews for AI-agent devices

Adopted  GRC impact ■■■ Low

MIIT's General Office published Notice No. 42 (dated 24 Oct) on network access for phones, tablets and wearables that ship with pre-installed AI agents. Before approval, devices must pass agent compatibility checks, a telecom-fraud risk assessment and anti-fraud testing, and each agent should carry a unique identity code based on national agent interconnection standards. Manufacturers must file a plan before over-the-air updates that add agent features and report afterwards, and app stores must publish compatibility lists and monitor the agents they list. The notice gives no effective date.

What it means for GRC: If you build or ship agent-enabled devices or apps for China, expect pre-market testing and formal change control on agent updates. It is also the first binding rule we have seen that gives every AI agent its own identity, a control worth copying into agent inventories and IAM.

2 · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom · 4 Nov 2026

EU AI Act starts to apply in Northern Ireland on 10 November

Guidance  GRC impact ■■■ Medium

The UK government published guidance on how the EU AI Act applies in Northern Ireland under the Windsor Framework from 10 November 2026. The application is limited and tied to goods covered by EU product rules; a newsletter summary of the guidance says standalone software is out of scope. The 32-page document helps providers, manufacturers, importers, distributors and users check whether their products are in scope and when the rules on prohibited, high-risk and limited-risk AI apply.

What it means for GRC: EU manufacturers already in scope get no new duties, but UK-based suppliers whose AI-enabled products reach the Northern Ireland market now have EU AI Act obligations. Check product portfolios and supply-chain roles (importer, distributor) for any Northern Ireland routes.

3 · 🇪🇺 European Union · 3 Nov 2026

CJEU Advocate General: chatbot answers can infringe press publishers' rights

Guidance  GRC impact ■■■ Medium

In Like Company v Google (C-250/25), Advocate General Szpunar advised that a chatbot which fetches a press article and reuses it in an answer may infringe the publisher's right. That depends on whether the article was accessed lawfully (for example, without getting around a paywall) and whether the answer copies the article's wording rather than just its facts. He called the questions on model training inadmissible here, but suggested training is generally covered by the text and data mining exception. The provider would be liable where such reuse is built into its business model or not credibly countered. The Opinion is not binding, and the Court has not yet ruled.

What it means for GRC: If you run retrieval-based chatbots over news or other paywalled content, review how sources are accessed, how outputs are filtered and what licences you hold. Add the case to the IP section of your AI risk register until the judgment arrives.

4 · 🇰🇷 South Korea · 6 Nov 2026

KISA publishes a zero-trust security guide for agentic AI

Guidance  GRC impact ■■■ Medium

The Korea Internet & Security Agency released an Agentic AI Zero Trust Guide to help companies apply zero-trust principles where AI agents act inside their systems. It builds on Korean and international AI security and zero-trust guidance and was developed with experts from industry, academia and research. The guide is voluntary.

What it means for GRC: A free reference for checking how core zero-trust ideas (strong identity, least privilege, continuous verification) carry over to agents, alongside OWASP and NIST material. Use it to test your agent access-control design before auditors ask how agents are governed under NIS2 or the AI Act.

Source: KISA →

5 · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom · 3 Nov 2026

NCSC: AI-powered ransomware against critical infrastructure highly likely by 2028

Guidance  GRC impact ■■■ Medium

The NCSC's annual State of the Cyber Nation report judges it highly likely that AI-powered malware will be used in a ransomware attack on UK critical national infrastructure by 2028. Within two years, it expects hostile use of AI to make ransomware, social engineering and vulnerability exploitation larger and faster, mainly by exploiting old weaknesses at scale rather than through new techniques. The NCSC urges organisations to invest properly in security, keep systems off the internet unless needed, and treat Cyber Essentials as a baseline only.

What it means for GRC: Use the assessment to make the case for faster patching and attack-surface reduction in risk committees. Swedish operators under NIS2 face the same trend, so add AI-accelerated exploitation to threat scenarios and resilience tests.

Source: NCSC →

6 · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom · 4 Nov 2026

UK plans law and independent watchdog for police facial recognition

Proposed  GRC impact ■■■ Low

After a public consultation, the Home Office said it will legislate so that police use of facial recognition is intelligence-led and proportionate. A new independent regulator could seek court injunctions against forces that do not comply, and systems would need independent bias testing. Legal codes of practice will follow for the police and, separately, for private-sector users such as shops. No bill has been published yet.

What it means for GRC: Shows where UK biometric AI oversight is heading, including private-sector use. Retailers with UK sites that use facial recognition should expect a statutory code, while EU deployers already face AI Act limits on remote biometric identification.

Source: GOV.UK →
